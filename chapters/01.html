SI units & fundamental constants

Thermodynamics. Temperature \(T\) in kelvins \(\mathrm{K}\); internal energy \(U\), microstate energy \(E_i\), work \(W\), heat \(Q\), free energies \(F,G\), and chemical potential \(\mu\) in joules \(\mathrm{J}\) (chemical potential is energy per particle); entropy \(S\) and heat capacity \(C_V,C_P\) in \(\mathrm{J/K}\); pressure \(P\) in pascals \(\mathrm{Pa}=\mathrm{J/m^3}\); volume \(V\) in \(\mathrm{m^3}\).

Statistical quantities. Particle count \(N\), multiplicity \(\Omega\), degeneracy \(g\), probability \(p_i\), occupation \(\bar n_i\), and properly normalized partition functions \(Z,\Xi\) are dimensionless; \(\beta=1/(k_BT)\) has units \(\mathrm{J^{-1}}\). Energy variance has units \(\mathrm{J^2}\).

Exact SI constants.

\[\boxed{k_B=1.380649\times10^{-23}\ \mathrm{J/K}},\qquad \boxed{h=6.62607015\times10^{-34}\ \mathrm{J\,s}}\]

\(k_B\) (Boltzmann's constant) and \(h\) (Planck's constant) have exact defined SI values. The reduced Planck constant is \(\hbar=h/(2\pi)\approx1.054571817\times10^{-34}\ \mathrm{J\,s}\).

Quantum and gases. Mass \(m\) in \(\mathrm{kg}\); length \(L\), thermal wavelength \(\lambda_T\) in \(\mathrm{m}\); angular frequency \(\omega\) in \(\mathrm{rad/s}\); energy gap \(\Delta\), single-particle level \(\epsilon\) in \(\mathrm{J}\); speed \(v\) in \(\mathrm{m/s}\); number density \(n=N/V_d\) in \(\mathrm{m^{-d}}\) for \(d\) spatial dimensions.

Common distinctions. \(T\) in formulas for temperature is kelvins \(\mathrm{K}\); magnetic flux density \(B\) is in tesla \(\mathrm{T}\). A magnetic moment \(\mu_{\rm mag}\) has units \(\mathrm{J/T}\), different from chemical potential \(\mu\) in \(\mathrm{J}\). Logarithm/exponential arguments and quantities such as \(k_BT/(\hbar\omega)\) must be dimensionless.

01.1 · Fundamental laws

Heat, work, and the first law

SI units: \(Q,W,U,H,F,G\) in \(\mathrm{J}\); \(\delta Q,\delta W,dU\) in \(\mathrm{J}\); \(P\) in \(\mathrm{Pa}\), \(dV\) in \(\mathrm{m^3}\), so \(P\,dV\) is in \(\mathrm{J}\). Temperature \(T\) is in \(\mathrm{K}\), entropy \(S\) in \(\mathrm{J/K}\), and chemical potential \(\mu\) in \(\mathrm{J}\) per particle.

Choose the sign convention \(Q>0\) means heat into the system, while \(W>0\) means work done by the system. For a closed system,

\[\boxed{\Delta U=Q-W},\qquad \boxed{dU=\delta Q-\delta W}.\]

The different symbols matter: \(U\) is a state function (exact differential), whereas heat and work depend on the process path. For quasistatic mechanical pressure work, \(\delta W=P_{\mathrm{ext}}\,dV\); equality with \(P\,dV\) requires mechanical reversibility.

Principle before proof: the first law

Statement. The first law is conservation of energy applied to a thermodynamic system: internal energy changes through energy transfers such as heat and work. Heat and work are not stored substances; they name ways energy crosses the system boundary. Consequently the same initial and final states may be connected by paths with different heat and work, even though \(\Delta U\) is the same.

Why it makes sense. A gas can become warmer by absorbing heat or by being compressed. Both change the same state function \(U\). The sign convention matters: here \(Q\) is heat entering and \(W\) is work done by the system.

Assumptions and use. The simple \(\Delta U=Q-W\) expression applies to a closed system with no additional energy-transfer modes. For open systems include transported matter and other relevant energy terms. The equilibrium identity \(dU=T\,dS-P\,dV+\mu\,dN\) is a relation among neighboring equilibrium states, not permission to write \(\delta Q=T\,dS\) on an irreversible path.

Derive the equilibrium fundamental identity

For a reversible step in a simple compressible single-component system, \(\delta Q_{\rm rev}=T\,dS\) and \(\delta W_{\rm rev}=P\,dV\).

\[dU=T\,dS-P\,dV.\]

If matter can also be added, the energy change associated with \(dN\) particles is \(\mu\,dN\):

\[\boxed{dU=T\,dS-P\,dV+\mu\,dN}.\]

This identity relates neighboring equilibrium states even if an actual path connecting them is irreversible; one must not identify \(\delta Q\) with \(T\,dS\) for an irreversible path.

Read off natural derivatives:

\[T=\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial S}\right)_{V,N},\quad P=-\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial V}\right)_{S,N},\quad \mu=\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial N}\right)_{S,V}.\]

01.2 · Second law

Entropy and reversibility

SI units: Entropy \(S,\Delta S\) in \(\mathrm{J/K}\); \(\delta Q_{\rm rev}/T\) in \(\mathrm{J/K}\); \(N\) dimensionless, \(k_B\) in \(\mathrm{J/K}\). Volume ratios such as \(V_f/V_i\) must be dimensionless.

\[dS\ge\frac{\delta Q}{T_{\mathrm{boundary}}}\quad\text{(suitable closed-system heat exchange)},\] \[\boxed{\Delta S_{\mathrm{isolated}}\ge0}.\]

Entropy is not a synonym for heat. For a reversible transfer \(dS=\delta Q_{\rm rev}/T\); in an irreversible process, additional entropy is generated internally.

Principle before proof: the second law

Statement. For an isolated system the total entropy cannot decrease: \(\Delta S_{\mathrm{isolated}}\ge0\). Entropy can increase without heat entering. For a system exchanging heat, total entropy generation must also be considered; reversible heat exchange gives \(\delta Q_{\mathrm{rev}}=T\,dS\).

Physical meaning. The second law singles out the direction of spontaneous processes. For instance, an initially localized ideal gas spreads to occupy a larger accessible volume because the expanded macrostate corresponds to many more accessible microscopic arrangements.

Conditions and caution. An isolated whole system is essential to the simple nondecrease statement. Entropy of a subsystem may decrease when it exports enough entropy to its surroundings. Reversible and irreversible paths can connect the same endpoint states, so entropy change can be calculated using a convenient reversible reference path even when the actual process is irreversible.

Worked example · free expansion of an ideal gas

An insulated ideal gas expands freely from \(V_i\) to \(V_f\) into vacuum. For the actual process, \(Q=0\) and \(W=0\), hence \(\Delta U=0\). For an ideal gas \(U=U(T)\), thus \(T_f=T_i\).

To evaluate the state function \(\Delta S\), imagine a reversible isothermal path connecting the same initial and final equilibrium states:

\[\Delta S=\int_i^f \frac{\delta Q_{\rm rev}}{T}=\int_{V_i}^{V_f}\frac{P\,dV}{T}.\]

Use \(P=Nk_BT/V\):

\[\boxed{\Delta S=Nk_B\ln\left(\frac{V_f}{V_i}\right)>0}\quad(V_f>V_i).\]

Entropy increases although no heat enters during the actual irreversible expansion.

Answer unit check: \(\Delta S=Nk_B\ln(V_f/V_i)\) has units \(\mathrm{J/K}\); the logarithm's volume ratio carries no units.

01.3 · Legendre transforms

Thermodynamic potentials and natural variables

SI units: \(H,F,G,\Omega_{\rm G}\) in \(\mathrm{J}\); \(T\,dS\) in \(\mathrm{K}\times\mathrm{J/K}=\mathrm{J}\); \(P\,dV\) in \(\mathrm{Pa\,m^3}=\mathrm{J}\); \(\mu\,dN\) in \(\mathrm{J}\).

Derive the differentials of \(H,F,G\)

Begin with \(dU=T\,dS-P\,dV+\mu\,dN\). Introduce enthalpy \(H=U+PV\):

\[dH=dU+P\,dV+V\,dP=\boxed{T\,dS+V\,dP+\mu\,dN}.\]

Define Helmholtz free energy \(F=U-TS\):

\[dF=dU-T\,dS-S\,dT=\boxed{-S\,dT-P\,dV+\mu\,dN}.\]

Define Gibbs free energy \(G=U+PV-TS\):

\[dG=dU+P\,dV+V\,dP-T\,dS-S\,dT=\boxed{-S\,dT+V\,dP+\mu\,dN}.\]

Natural variables: \(U(S,V,N)\), \(H(S,P,N)\), \(F(T,V,N)\), and \(G(T,P,N)\). A Legendre transform exchanges a variable (such as \(S\)) for its conjugate variable (such as \(T\)).

Derive the grand potential and equilibrium criterion

For variable particle number at fixed \(T,V,\mu\), use \(\Omega_{\rm G}=F-\mu N=U-TS-\mu N\). Differentiate:

\[d\Omega_{\rm G}=dF-\mu\,dN-N\,d\mu,\] \[\boxed{d\Omega_{\rm G}=-S\,dT-P\,dV-N\,d\mu}.\]

At fixed \(T,V,N\), equilibrium minimizes \(F\) when only allowed mechanical/internal variations occur. At fixed \(T,P,N\), the appropriate minimum principle is for \(G\); at fixed \(T,V,\mu\), it is for \(\Omega_{\rm G}\). These statements require the relevant reservoir constraints and stable equilibrium.

01.4 · Mixed derivatives

Derive a Maxwell relation

SI units: The two sides of \((\partial S/\partial V)_{T,N}=(\partial P/\partial T)_{V,N}\) have units \(\mathrm{J/(K\,m^3)}=\mathrm{Pa/K}\).

Principle before proof: Maxwell relations

Statement. Maxwell relations connect otherwise different-looking thermodynamic derivatives. They follow from the equality of mixed second derivatives of a smooth thermodynamic potential. For example, from \(F(T,V,N)\), \((\partial S/\partial V)_{T,N}=(\partial P/\partial T)_{V,N}\).

Physical meaning. A change in entropy caused by changing volume at fixed temperature is linked to how pressure changes with temperature at fixed volume. This lets one replace a difficult-to-measure derivative with an experimentally or theoretically more accessible one.

Validity and use. The system must be in equilibrium and the potential sufficiently smooth in the region considered; carefully retain the listed fixed variables. At first-order phase transitions, derivatives may be discontinuous and the naive smooth-derivative argument cannot be applied across the transition. Use the potential whose natural variables match the problem.

From \(dF\) to \(\partial_V S=\partial_T P\)

At fixed \(N\), \(dF=-S\,dT-P\,dV\), hence

\[S=-\left(\frac{\partial F}{\partial T}\right)_{V,N},\quad P=-\left(\frac{\partial F}{\partial V}\right)_{T,N}.\]

Differentiate the first expression with respect to \(V\) at fixed \(T,N\), and the second with respect to \(T\) at fixed \(V,N\). Smoothness of \(F\) ensures its mixed derivatives commute:

\[\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial V}\right)_{T,N} =-\frac{\partial^2F}{\partial V\partial T} =\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial T}\right)_{V,N}.\] \[\boxed{\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial V}\right)_{T,N} =\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial T}\right)_{V,N}}.\]

Other Maxwell relations follow in the same manner from \(U,H,G\). A Maxwell relation is not an additional law: it follows from the existence of a smooth thermodynamic potential.

01.5 · Response functions

Heat capacities and adiabatic ideal-gas process

SI units: \(C_V,C_P\) in \(\mathrm{J/K}\) for the whole system, or \(\mathrm{J/(mol\,K)}\) if explicitly molar. The ratio \(\gamma=C_P/C_V\) and powers of volume ratios are dimensionless.

\[\boxed{C_V=\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial T}\right)_{V,N}},\qquad \boxed{C_P=\left(\frac{\partial H}{\partial T}\right)_{P,N}}.\]

For a classical monatomic ideal gas in 3D, \(U=\frac32Nk_BT\), \(C_V=\frac32Nk_B\), \(C_P=\frac52Nk_B\), and \(C_P-C_V=Nk_B\).

Principle before proof: heat capacities and reversible adiabats

Statement. A heat capacity measures the change in the system's energy under a specified thermal constraint: \(C_V=(\partial U/\partial T)_{V,N}\), while \(C_P=(\partial H/\partial T)_{P,N}\). Holding pressure rather than volume fixed can allow expansion work, so the two are generally different.

Physical interpretation. A larger \(C_V\) means more energy is needed per kelvin at fixed volume. For a classical monatomic ideal gas, the three translational quadratic kinetic terms give \(C_V=3Nk_B/2\). The relation \(PV^\gamma=\mathrm{constant}\) describes how pressure changes during a reversible adiabatic ideal-gas process, not an arbitrary insulated process.

Assumptions. For the stated adiabatic power laws the gas obeys the ideal-gas equation, its heat capacities are constant in the temperature interval, the process is quasistatic and reversible, and there is no heat transfer. Free expansion is insulated but irreversible and does not follow that power law.

Derive \(TV^{\gamma-1}=\mathrm{constant}\) for a reversible adiabat

For a reversible adiabatic (\(\delta Q=0\)), first law gives \(dU=-P\,dV\). For an ideal gas with constant \(C_V\), \(dU=C_VdT\), and \(P=Nk_BT/V\).

\[C_V\,dT=-\frac{Nk_BT}{V}\,dV.\]

Divide by \(T\), and integrate:

\[\frac{dT}{T}=-\frac{Nk_B}{C_V}\frac{dV}{V} \ \Longrightarrow\ \ln T+\frac{Nk_B}{C_V}\ln V=\mathrm{constant}.\]

With \(\gamma=C_P/C_V\) and \(Nk_B=C_P-C_V\),

\[\boxed{TV^{\gamma-1}=\mathrm{constant}},\qquad \boxed{PV^\gamma=\mathrm{constant}}.\]

Free expansion is adiabatic but not a reversible adiabat, so this \(PV^\gamma\) law should not be used for it.

Answer unit check: Both \(TV^{\gamma-1}=\mathrm{constant}\) and \(PV^\gamma=\mathrm{constant}\) use the same units on each side of a comparison between states; the constants themselves generally have dimensional units, so use ratios if taking logarithms.
