09.1 · Assumptions

What “low temperature” means

A system is quantum or classical relative to the energy scales of the states it can access; dimensionality alone is not sufficient. A discrete gap \(\Delta\) defines \(y=\Delta/(k_BT)\).

\[\begin{aligned} k_BT\ll\Delta&:\ \text{ground-state dominated},\\ k_BT\gg\Delta&:\ \text{many levels accessible}. \end{aligned}\]

For a many-particle gas, a further condition governs quantum degeneracy: \(n\lambda_T^d\) small or large (with density \(n=N/V_d\) and thermal wavelength \(\lambda_T=h/\sqrt{2\pi mk_BT}\)). At high temperature with \(n\lambda_T^d\ll1\), Bose/Fermi gases approach Maxwell–Boltzmann statistics.

09.2 · Harmonic oscillators

Exact low/high \(T\) heat capacity in 1D–3D

Derive \(U\) and \(C_V\) for a 1D oscillator

Let \(x=\beta\hbar\omega\). For one oscillator,

\[Z=\frac{e^{-x/2}}{1-e^{-x}},\qquad \ln Z=-\frac x2-\ln(1-e^{-x}).\]

Differentiate with respect to \(\beta\), noting \(dx/d\beta=\hbar\omega\):

\[\frac{\partial\ln Z}{\partial\beta}=-\frac{\hbar\omega}{2}-\frac{\hbar\omega e^{-x}}{1-e^{-x}}.\] \[\boxed{U=\hbar\omega\left(\frac12+\frac{1}{e^x-1}\right)}.\]

For \(C_V=dU/dT\), \(dx/dT=-x/T\). Thus

\[\frac{dU}{dx}=-\hbar\omega\frac{e^x}{(e^x-1)^2},\] \[\boxed{C_V=k_Bx^2\frac{e^x}{(e^x-1)^2}}.\]

Low \(T\): \(x\gg1\), \(1/(e^x-1)\approx e^{-x}\), so

\[U\approx\frac{\hbar\omega}{2}+\hbar\omega e^{-x},\quad C_V\approx k_Bx^2e^{-x}\to0.\]

High \(T\): \(x\ll1\), \(1/(e^x-1)=x^{-1}-1/2+x/12+\cdots\), giving

\[U=k_BT+\frac{(\hbar\omega)^2}{12k_BT}+\cdots,\quad C_V\to k_B.\]

The high-temperature energy agrees with equipartition: one quadratic kinetic and one quadratic potential term contribute \(k_BT/2\) each. The quantum ground energy is included throughout.

2D/3D anisotropic oscillators: sum the independent modes

For frequencies \(\omega_j\), \(j=1,\ldots,d\), separation gives \(Z_d=\prod_j Z_j\), hence \(\ln Z_d=\sum_j\ln Z_j\).

\[\boxed{U_d=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\hbar\omega_j\left(\frac12+\frac1{e^{x_j}-1}\right)},\quad x_j=\frac{\hbar\omega_j}{k_BT}.\] \[\boxed{C_{V,d}=k_B\sum_{j=1}^{d}x_j^2\frac{e^{x_j}}{(e^{x_j}-1)^2}}.\]

For \(d=2\), sum the \(x\) and \(y\) terms; for \(d=3\), also include \(z\). At \(T\to0\), \(U_d\to\frac12\sum_j\hbar\omega_j\) and \(C_{V,d}\to0\). At \(k_BT\gg\hbar\omega_j\) for all modes,

\[\boxed{U_d\to d\,k_BT,\qquad C_{V,d}\to d\,k_B}.\]

When frequencies differ greatly, one mode may be classical while another remains quantum: use the exact sum rather than imposing one limit on every mode.

09.3 · Particles in boxes

Low-temperature freeze-out and classical continuum

Derive the ground state and first excitation gap in \(d\) dimensions

For one noninteracting particle in a \(d\)-dimensional cubic box of side \(L\), let \(\epsilon=\pi^2\hbar^2/(2mL^2)\). The energy is

\[E_{\mathbf n}=\epsilon\sum_{j=1}^d n_j^2,\qquad n_j=1,2,\ldots\]

The ground state has every \(n_j=1\), so \(E_0=d\epsilon\). The first excited manifold changes one \(n_j\) from 1 to 2, raising the energy by \((4-1)\epsilon=3\epsilon\) and giving degeneracy \(d\):

\[\boxed{E_0=d\epsilon,\qquad\Delta=3\epsilon,\qquad g_1=d}.\]

For \(k_BT\ll\Delta\), retain the ground state and first excited manifold in \(Z\):

\[Z\approx e^{-\beta E_0}(1+d e^{-\beta\Delta}+\cdots).\]

Then \(\ln Z\approx-\beta E_0+d e^{-\beta\Delta}\), and

\[\boxed{U\approx E_0+d\Delta e^{-\beta\Delta}},\qquad \boxed{C_V\approx d k_B(\beta\Delta)^2e^{-\beta\Delta}}.\]

Both the excess energy and heat capacity are exponentially suppressed near the nondegenerate ground state.

High-temperature limit: replace sums by Gaussian integrals

For one dimension, \(Z_1=\sum_{n=1}^\infty e^{-\beta\epsilon n^2}\). If many levels contribute, approximate the sum by an integral:

\[Z_1\approx\int_0^\infty e^{-\beta\epsilon n^2}\,dn =\frac12\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{\beta\epsilon}}.\]

Insert \(\epsilon=\pi^2\hbar^2/(2mL^2)\):

\[Z_1\approx \frac{L}{h}\sqrt{2\pi mk_BT}=\frac L{\lambda_T},\quad \boxed{\lambda_T=\frac h{\sqrt{2\pi mk_BT}}}.\]

For \(d\) independent Cartesian directions \(Z_d\approx(L/\lambda_T)^d\) (one particle, not \(N\) identical particles).

\[\ln Z_d\approx d\ln L-\frac d2\ln\beta+\mathrm{constant}.\]

Differentiate:

\[\boxed{U=-\partial_\beta\ln Z_d\approx\frac d{2\beta}=\frac d2 k_BT},\] \[\boxed{C_V\approx\frac d2k_B}.\]

Dimension dependence: a free translational degree contributes \(k_BT/2\), whereas an oscillator degree (kinetic plus quadratic potential) contributes \(k_BT\) when classical.

09.4 · Quantum statistics

When are Bose and Fermi gases classical?

Classical fugacity and occupation-number limit

For bosons or fermions at fugacity \(z=e^{\beta\mu}\), mean occupations are

\[\bar n_{\rm BE}=\frac1{z^{-1}e^{\beta\epsilon}-1},\qquad \bar n_{\rm FD}=\frac1{z^{-1}e^{\beta\epsilon}+1}.\]

If \(z e^{-\beta\epsilon}\ll1\) for the relevant levels, both denominators are dominated by \(z^{-1}e^{\beta\epsilon}\) and

\[\boxed{\bar n\approx z e^{-\beta\epsilon}}.\]

For a uniform dilute nonrelativistic gas, this regime corresponds approximately to

\[\boxed{n\lambda_T^d/g_s\ll1},\]

where \(g_s\) is spin degeneracy and \(n=N/V_d\). Low \(T\) does not always imply all particles occupy a single state: fermions fill many levels up to the Fermi energy at \(T=0\).

Summary: which high-\(T\) law applies?

\[\begin{array}{c|c|c} \text{One particle; }d\text{ dimensions}&U_{\text{high }T}&C_{V,\text{high }T}\\\hline \text{Free in box}&\frac d2k_BT&\frac d2 k_B\\ \text{Harmonic oscillator}&d k_BT&d k_B \end{array}\]

Equipartition is classical and requires the appropriate continuous phase-space description and quadratic Hamiltonian terms. Do not treat these limits as universal for all systems in \(d\) dimensions.
