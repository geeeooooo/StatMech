SI units & fundamental constants

Thermodynamics. Temperature \(T\) in kelvins \(\mathrm{K}\); internal energy \(U\), microstate energy \(E_i\), heat \(Q\), work \(W\), free energies and chemical potential \(\mu\) in joules \(\mathrm{J}\) (chemical potential is energy per particle); entropy \(S\) and heat capacity \(C_V,C_P\) in \(\mathrm{J/K}\); pressure \(P\) in pascals \(\mathrm{Pa}=\mathrm{J/m^3}\); volume \(V\) in \(\mathrm{m^3}\).

Statistical quantities. Particle count \(N\), multiplicity \(\Omega\), degeneracy \(g\), probability \(p_i\), occupation \(\bar n_i\), and properly normalized partition functions \(Z,\Xi\) are dimensionless; \(\beta=1/(k_BT)\) has units \(\mathrm{J^{-1}}\). Energy variance has units \(\mathrm{J^2}\).

Exact SI constants.

\[\boxed{k_B=1.380649\times10^{-23}\ \mathrm{J/K}},\qquad \boxed{h=6.62607015\times10^{-34}\ \mathrm{J\,s}}\]

\(k_B\) (Boltzmann's constant) and \(h\) (Planck's constant) have exact defined SI values. The reduced Planck constant is \(\hbar=h/(2\pi)\approx1.054571817\times10^{-34}\ \mathrm{J\,s}\).

Quantum and gases. Mass \(m\) in \(\mathrm{kg}\); length \(L\), thermal wavelength \(\lambda_T\) in \(\mathrm{m}\); angular frequency \(\omega\) in \(\mathrm{rad/s}\); energy gap \(\Delta\), single-particle level \(\epsilon\) in \(\mathrm{J}\); speed \(v\) in \(\mathrm{m/s}\); number density \(n=N/V_d\) in \(\mathrm{m^{-d}}\) for \(d\) spatial dimensions.

Common distinctions. \(T\) in formulas for temperature is kelvins \(\mathrm{K}\); magnetic flux density \(B\) is in tesla \(\mathrm{T}\). A magnetic moment \(\mu_{\rm mag}\) has units \(\mathrm{J/T}\), different from chemical potential \(\mu\) in \(\mathrm{J}\). Logarithm/exponential arguments and quantities such as \(k_BT/(\hbar\omega)\) must be dimensionless.

11.1 · Energy variance

Canonical energy fluctuations and heat capacity

SI units: Internal energy \(U\) in \(\mathrm{J}\), variance \(\operatorname{Var}(E)\) in \(\mathrm{J^2}\), \(C_V\) in \(\mathrm{J/K}\), \(\beta\) in \(\mathrm{J^{-1}}\).

The canonical ensemble has fixed \(T,V,N\), but fluctuating energy. A derivative of \(\ln Z\) relates those microscopic fluctuations to the thermodynamic heat capacity.

Derive \(\operatorname{Var}(E)=k_BT^2C_V\)

Start with \(Z=\sum_i e^{-\beta E_i}\) and \(U=\langle E\rangle=-\partial_\beta\ln Z\). First and second derivatives of \(Z\) are

\[Z'=-\sum_i E_i e^{-\beta E_i},\qquad Z''=\sum_i E_i^2e^{-\beta E_i}.\]

Using \((\ln Z)''=Z''/Z-(Z'/Z)^2\),

\[\frac{\partial^2\ln Z}{\partial\beta^2} =\langle E^2\rangle-\langle E\rangle^2 =\operatorname{Var}(E).\]

Differentiate \(U\) and use \(d\beta/dT=-1/(k_BT^2)\):

\[C_V=\frac{\partial U}{\partial T}= -\operatorname{Var}(E)\left(-\frac1{k_BT^2}\right).\] \[\boxed{\operatorname{Var}(E)=k_BT^2 C_V}.\]

Meaning: a system with a larger heat capacity exhibits larger equilibrium energy fluctuations at fixed \(T\). For ordinary canonical systems with fixed Hamiltonian \(C_V\ge0\).

Example · relative energy fluctuations of an ideal gas

For a 3D classical ideal monatomic gas, \(U=\frac32Nk_BT\) and \(C_V=\frac32Nk_B\). Therefore

\[\operatorname{Var}(E)=\frac32N(k_BT)^2.\]

The relative standard deviation is

\[\frac{\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(E)}}{U} =\frac{\sqrt{\frac32N}\,k_BT}{\frac32Nk_BT} =\boxed{\sqrt{\frac{2}{3N}}}.\]

The relative fluctuations vanish as \(N^{-1/2}\) for macroscopic systems, helping explain why ensemble averages predict sharply defined thermodynamic observables.

Answer unit check: \(\operatorname{Var}(E)\) is in \(\mathrm{J^2}\), while relative fluctuations are dimensionless.

11.2 · Particle variance

Grand canonical number fluctuations

SI units: Number \(N\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(N)\) dimensionless, chemical potential \(\mu\) and \(k_BT\) in \(\mathrm{J}\).

Derive \(\operatorname{Var}(N)=k_BT(\partial N/\partial\mu)\)

In the grand canonical ensemble, \(\Xi=\sum_a e^{-\beta(E_a-\mu N_a)}\). Let \(u=\beta\mu\). Then

\[\frac{\partial\ln\Xi}{\partial u}=\langle N\rangle,\quad \frac{\partial^2\ln\Xi}{\partial u^2} =\langle N^2\rangle-\langle N\rangle^2.\]

Because \(du/d\mu=\beta\) at fixed \(T,V\),

\[\frac{\partial\langle N\rangle}{\partial\mu} =\beta\operatorname{Var}(N).\] \[\boxed{\operatorname{Var}(N) =k_BT\left(\frac{\partial\langle N\rangle}{\partial\mu}\right)_{T,V}}.\]

Meaning: sensitivity of particle number to chemical potential is tied to the size of equilibrium number fluctuations.

11.3 · Response theorem

Fluctuation–response and susceptibility

SI units: If \(M\) is a magnetic moment in \(\mathrm{J/T}\) and coupling \(h\) a field in tesla \(\mathrm{T}\), \(\chi=\partial M/\partial h\) is in \(\mathrm{J/T^2}\). This differs from dimensionless volume susceptibility.

Derive \(\chi=\beta\,\operatorname{Var}(M)\)

Suppose an externally controlled field \(h\) couples to an observable \(M\) via the Hamiltonian \(H(h)=H_0-hM\), with \(M\) independent of \(h\). Then

\[Z(h)=\sum_i e^{-\beta(E_{0i}-hM_i)}.\]

Differentiate with respect to \(h\):

\[\frac{\partial\ln Z}{\partial h}=\beta\langle M\rangle.\]

Differentiate again, or apply the quotient rule to the weighted average:

\[\frac{\partial\langle M\rangle}{\partial h} =\beta(\langle M^2\rangle-\langle M\rangle^2).\] \[\boxed{\chi\equiv\left(\frac{\partial\langle M\rangle}{\partial h}\right)_T =\beta\operatorname{Var}(M)}.\]

The result assumes the specified linear coupling and canonical equilibrium; it need not hold unchanged for arbitrary field-dependent observables or non-equilibrium systems.

Worked example · one classical two-state magnetic moment

Consider a moment with \(M=\pm\mu\) and energies \(E=-hM=\mp\mu h\). Then

\[Z=e^{\beta\mu h}+e^{-\beta\mu h}=2\cosh(\beta\mu h).\] \[\langle M\rangle=\frac1\beta\partial_h\ln Z =\mu\tanh(\beta\mu h).\]

Differentiate once more:

\[\boxed{\chi=\beta\mu^2\,\operatorname{sech}^2(\beta\mu h)}.\]

At zero field, \(\chi_0=\mu^2/(k_BT)\); for \(N\) noninteracting identical spins multiply by \(N\). The inverse-temperature behavior is the Curie-law form for this simple model.

Answer unit check: Total-moment susceptibility \(\chi\) is in \(\mathrm{J/T^2}\) for a magnetic field measured in tesla.

11.4 · Stability

Free-energy minimization and phase coexistence

SI units: Gibbs energy \(G\) and \(\mu\,dN\) in \(\mathrm{J}\), \(T\) in \(\mathrm{K}\), \(P\) in \(\mathrm{Pa}\), chemical potential \(\mu\) in \(\mathrm{J}\) per particle.

At fixed \(T,V,N\), a stable equilibrium minimizes Helmholtz \(F\) over accessible macroscopic configurations. At fixed \(T,P,N\), minimize Gibbs \(G\). Local stability requires appropriate positive curvature with respect to allowed order-parameter variations.

Derive equality of chemical potentials in coexisting phases

Two phases \(\alpha,\beta\) are in thermal and mechanical equilibrium at the same \(T,P\), and may exchange particles while total \(N=N_\alpha+N_\beta\) remains fixed.

For variations of a single-component system at fixed \(T,P\), \(dG_i=\mu_i\,dN_i\). Therefore

\[dG_{\rm tot}=\mu_\alpha dN_\alpha+\mu_\beta dN_\beta.\]

Conservation gives \(dN_\beta=-dN_\alpha\), so

\[dG_{\rm tot}=(\mu_\alpha-\mu_\beta)dN_\alpha.\]

At coexistence, the first variation must vanish for allowed exchange, yielding

\[\boxed{\mu_\alpha(T,P)=\mu_\beta(T,P)}.\]

This is one chemical-equilibrium condition for coexisting phases, alongside equal temperature and pressure.

11.5 · Advanced extension

Landau theory: a simple mean-field transition

SI units: Free energy \(F\) in \(\mathrm{J}\), critical temperature \(T_c\) in \(\mathrm{K}\). For dimensionless order parameter \(m\), \(a\) in \(\mathrm{J/K}\), \(b\) in \(\mathrm{J}\); this \(m\) is not mass.

Minimize an even fourth-order free-energy model

Consider an order parameter \(m\) and a phenomenological free energy, where \(a,b>0\):

\[F(m,T)=F_0+\frac a2(T-T_c)m^2+\frac b4m^4.\]

Stationary states satisfy

\[\frac{\partial F}{\partial m}=a(T-T_c)m+bm^3=0,\] \[m[a(T-T_c)+bm^2]=0.\]

Solutions are \(m=0\), and if \(T<T_c\), \(m=\pm\sqrt{a(T_c-T)/b}\). The second derivative is

\[\frac{\partial^2F}{\partial m^2}=a(T-T_c)+3bm^2.\]

At \(T>T_c\), \(m=0\) is stable. At \(T<T_c\), \(m=0\) becomes unstable and the two nonzero extrema are stable:

\[\boxed{m_{\rm eq}=\pm\sqrt{\frac{a(T_c-T)}{b}}\quad (T<T_c)}.\]

This continuous symmetry-breaking transition is a mean-field model, not a universal exact result. Critical exponents and fluctuations can differ in real systems, especially in low dimensions.
