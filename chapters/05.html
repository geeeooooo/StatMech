05.1 · Boundary conditions

1D infinite well: why \(n=1,2,\ldots\)

A particle of mass \(m\) occupies \(0<x<L\), with \(V(x)=0\) inside and infinite potential outside. The wavefunction must vanish at both impenetrable walls; normalized states must be nonzero.

Derive quantized \(k_n\), wavefunctions, and energies

Inside the well, the stationary Schrödinger equation is

\[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2}=E\psi \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \psi''+k^2\psi=0,\quad k^2=\frac{2mE}{\hbar^2}.\]

Its real-wavenumber solution is \(\psi(x)=A\sin kx+B\cos kx\).

At \(x=0\), \(\psi(0)=0\Rightarrow B=0\). At \(x=L\), \(\psi(L)=A\sin(kL)=0\).

The nonzero eigenfunction requires \(A\ne0\), so \(\sin(kL)=0\):

\[kL=n\pi,\qquad k_n=\frac{n\pi}{L}.\]

For \(n=0\), \(k=0\). The correct \(E=0\) solution of the differential equation is \(\psi=Ax+B\). The two wall conditions give \(B=0\) and \(AL=0\Rightarrow A=0\). Thus \(n=0\) yields only the zero vector, which cannot be normalized or represent a state. Negative \(n\) merely relabels the same sine modes up to sign.

Normalize, using \(\int_0^L\sin^2(n\pi x/L)\,dx=L/2\):

\[\boxed{\psi_n(x)=\sqrt{\frac2L}\sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right)},\qquad n=1,2,\ldots\] \[\boxed{E_n=\frac{n^2\pi^2\hbar^2}{2mL^2}}.\]

Physical meaning: the walls enforce nodes, and the lowest allowed mode has a finite wavelength and energy. Ground-state energy is not zero even though the potential is zero inside.

05.2 · Ladder operators

Quantum harmonic oscillator: why \(n=0,1,\ldots\)

For \(V(x)=\frac12m\omega^2x^2\), the wavefunction is not forced to vanish at any finite wall. Instead, acceptable states must be square-integrable on the whole line.

Derive the energy spectrum from \(a^\dagger a\)

Define dimensionless ladder operators

\[a=\sqrt{\frac{m\omega}{2\hbar}}x+\frac{i}{\sqrt{2m\hbar\omega}}p,\qquad a^\dagger=\sqrt{\frac{m\omega}{2\hbar}}x-\frac{i}{\sqrt{2m\hbar\omega}}p.\]

Using \([x,p]=i\hbar\), one finds \([a,a^\dagger]=1\). Rewriting \(H=p^2/(2m)+m\omega^2x^2/2\),

\[\boxed{H=\hbar\omega\left(a^\dagger a+\frac12\right)}.\]

Define number operator \(N=a^\dagger a\). Since \(\langle\psi|N|\psi\rangle=\|a|\psi\rangle\|^2\ge0\), \(N\) has nonnegative eigenvalues.

The commutators \([N,a]=-a\), \([N,a^\dagger]=a^\dagger\) show that \(a\) lowers and \(a^\dagger\) raises the eigenvalue of \(N\) by one. Repeated lowering must end at a lowest normalizable state \(|0\rangle\) satisfying \(a|0\rangle=0\); its eigenvalue is zero. Raising gives \(n=0,1,2,\ldots\).

\[\boxed{E_n=\hbar\omega\left(n+\frac12\right)},\qquad n=0,1,2,\ldots\]

Not a contradiction: the oscillator has an admissible state labeled \(n=0\), but its energy is still \(E_0=\frac12\hbar\omega>0\).

Derive the ground-state Gaussian explicitly

In position space \(p=-i\hbar\,d/dx\). Applying \(a\psi_0=0\) gives

\[\left(\sqrt{\frac{m\omega}{2\hbar}}x+\sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{2m\omega}}\frac{d}{dx}\right)\psi_0=0,\] \[\frac{d\psi_0}{dx}=-\frac{m\omega}{\hbar}x\psi_0.\]

Separate variables and integrate:

\[\frac{d\psi_0}{\psi_0}=-\frac{m\omega}{\hbar}x\,dx,\qquad \psi_0=C\,e^{-m\omega x^2/(2\hbar)}.\]

Normalization \(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|\psi_0|^2dx=1\), together with the Gaussian integral, gives

\[\boxed{\psi_0(x)=\left(\frac{m\omega}{\pi\hbar}\right)^{1/4}e^{-m\omega x^2/(2\hbar)}}.\]

The Gaussian is nonzero and normalizable, so \(n=0\) is a genuine state.

05.3 · Separable systems

2D and 3D spectra and degeneracy

Rectangular box · why every \(n_j\ge1\)

For a rectangular box of side lengths \(L_j\), separation \(\psi(\mathbf r)=\prod_jX_j(x_j)\) yields independent sine modes with \(n_j=1,2,\ldots\). The total energy is additive:

\[\boxed{E_{\{n_j\}}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{\pi^2\hbar^2n_j^2}{2mL_j^2}},\qquad d=1,2,3.\]

For a cubic box the ground state is \((1,1,\ldots,1)\) and first excited levels are degenerate: any one of \(d\) coordinates may change from \(1\) to \(2\).

2D anisotropic oscillator · derive the energies

Take a separable Hamiltonian

\[H=\frac{p_x^2+p_y^2}{2m}+\frac12m\omega_x^2x^2+\frac12m\omega_y^2y^2=H_x+H_y.\]

Each 1D factor has \(E_{n_x}^{(x)}=\hbar\omega_x(n_x+1/2)\) and \(E_{n_y}^{(y)}=\hbar\omega_y(n_y+1/2)\). Because the factors act on separate coordinates and commute, the product wavefunction is an energy eigenstate:

\[\boxed{E_{n_x,n_y}=\hbar\omega_x(n_x+\tfrac12)+\hbar\omega_y(n_y+\tfrac12)},\] \[\boxed{n_x,n_y=0,1,2,\ldots}.\]

At generic unequal (incommensurate) frequencies, degeneracy is usually absent. For an isotropic 2D oscillator, \(\omega_x=\omega_y=\omega\), \(E=\hbar\omega(N+1)\) where \(N=n_x+n_y\), and degeneracy is \(N+1\).

For isotropic 3D, \(N=n_x+n_y+n_z\), \(E=\hbar\omega(N+3/2)\), with degeneracy \(g_N=(N+1)(N+2)/2\).

05.4 · Connecting spectra to thermodynamics

Quantum single-particle partition functions

Oscillator partition function: geometric series

For one oscillator, insert \(E_n=\hbar\omega(n+1/2)\) into the canonical sum:

\[Z_{\rm osc}=\sum_{n=0}^\infty e^{-\beta\hbar\omega(n+1/2)} =e^{-\beta\hbar\omega/2}\sum_{n=0}^\infty(e^{-\beta\hbar\omega})^n.\]

Use \(\sum_{n=0}^\infty q^n=1/(1-q)\) for \(|q|<1\):

\[\boxed{Z_{\rm osc}=\frac{e^{-\beta\hbar\omega/2}}{1-e^{-\beta\hbar\omega}}}.\]

For separable oscillators in \(d\) dimensions, multiply independent factors:

\[\boxed{Z_d=\prod_{j=1}^d\frac{e^{-\beta\hbar\omega_j/2}}{1-e^{-\beta\hbar\omega_j}}}.\]
Infinite well partition function: why the sum starts at 1

The 1D box has \(E_n=\epsilon n^2\) with \(\epsilon=\pi^2\hbar^2/(2mL^2)\), \(n=1,2,\ldots\). Therefore

\[\boxed{Z_{\rm box}^{(1)}=\sum_{n=1}^\infty e^{-\beta\epsilon n^2}}.\]

Unlike the oscillator spectrum, the energies scale quadratically with \(n\), so the sum is not an elementary geometric series. For a separable rectangular box \(Z_d=\prod_j\sum_{n_j=1}^\infty\exp[-\beta\pi^2\hbar^2n_j^2/(2mL_j^2)]\).

Exam summary

\[\begin{array}{c|c|c} \text{System}&\text{Allowed }n&\text{Ground energy}\\\hline \text{Infinite well}&1,2,\dots&\pi^2\hbar^2/(2mL^2)\\ \text{Harmonic oscillator}&0,1,\dots&\hbar\omega/2 \end{array}\]

Quantum-number labels differ because the operator domains and boundary/normalization conditions differ, not because one system violates the zero-point principle.
