SI units & fundamental constants

Thermodynamics. Temperature \(T\) in kelvins \(\mathrm{K}\); internal energy \(U\), microstate energy \(E_i\), heat \(Q\), work \(W\), free energies and chemical potential \(\mu\) in joules \(\mathrm{J}\) (chemical potential is energy per particle); entropy \(S\) and heat capacity \(C_V,C_P\) in \(\mathrm{J/K}\); pressure \(P\) in pascals \(\mathrm{Pa}=\mathrm{J/m^3}\); volume \(V\) in \(\mathrm{m^3}\).

Statistical quantities. Particle count \(N\), multiplicity \(\Omega\), degeneracy \(g\), probability \(p_i\), occupation \(\bar n_i\), and properly normalized partition functions \(Z,\Xi\) are dimensionless; \(\beta=1/(k_BT)\) has units \(\mathrm{J^{-1}}\). Energy variance has units \(\mathrm{J^2}\).

Exact SI constants.

\[\boxed{k_B=1.380649\times10^{-23}\ \mathrm{J/K}},\qquad \boxed{h=6.62607015\times10^{-34}\ \mathrm{J\,s}}\]

\(k_B\) (Boltzmann's constant) and \(h\) (Planck's constant) have exact defined SI values. The reduced Planck constant is \(\hbar=h/(2\pi)\approx1.054571817\times10^{-34}\ \mathrm{J\,s}\).

Quantum and gases. Mass \(m\) in \(\mathrm{kg}\); length \(L\), thermal wavelength \(\lambda_T\) in \(\mathrm{m}\); angular frequency \(\omega\) in \(\mathrm{rad/s}\); energy gap \(\Delta\), single-particle level \(\epsilon\) in \(\mathrm{J}\); speed \(v\) in \(\mathrm{m/s}\); number density \(n=N/V_d\) in \(\mathrm{m^{-d}}\) for \(d\) spatial dimensions.

Common distinctions. \(T\) in formulas for temperature is kelvins \(\mathrm{K}\); magnetic flux density \(B\) is in tesla \(\mathrm{T}\). A magnetic moment \(\mu_{\rm mag}\) has units \(\mathrm{J/T}\), different from chemical potential \(\mu\) in \(\mathrm{J}\). Logarithm/exponential arguments and quantities such as \(k_BT/(\hbar\omega)\) must be dimensionless.

07.1 · Identical quantum particles

Why occupation statistics differ

SI units: Single-particle levels \(\epsilon_j\), chemical potential \(\mu\) in \(\mathrm{J}\); inverse temperature \(\beta\) in \(\mathrm{J^{-1}}\); occupations \(n_j\), fugacity \(z\), and \(\Xi\) dimensionless.

For independent single-particle levels \(\epsilon_j\), a many-body microstate is specified by occupations \(n_j\). Bosons permit \(n_j=0,1,2,\ldots\); spinless fermions or one fermionic spin-orbital permit \(n_j=0,1\). The particle number and energy are \(N=\sum_jn_j\), \(E=\sum_jn_j\epsilon_j\).

\[\boxed{\Xi=\prod_j\Xi_j},\qquad \Xi_j=\sum_{n_j}e^{-\beta(\epsilon_j-\mu)n_j}.\]

Factorization assumes a noninteracting gas in the grand canonical ensemble. Different spin states correspond to different orbitals; a level of degeneracy \(g_j\) includes \(g_j\) independent orbitals.

07.2 · Bosons

Derive Bose–Einstein occupation

SI units: Orbital energy \(\epsilon\) and \(\mu\) in \(\mathrm{J}\); \(T\) in \(\mathrm{K}\); exponent \(\beta(\epsilon-\mu)\) and mean occupation \(\bar n_{\rm BE}\) dimensionless.

Grand sum over all nonnegative occupancies

Let \(y=e^{-\beta(\epsilon-\mu)}=ze^{-\beta\epsilon}\), with fugacity \(z=e^{\beta\mu}\). For a bosonic orbital,

\[\Xi_\epsilon=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}y^n=\frac1{1-y}\qquad (y<1).\]

The mean occupation follows by differentiating the log partition function with respect to \(u=\beta\mu\):

\[\bar n=\frac{\partial\ln\Xi_\epsilon}{\partial u} =\frac{\partial[-\ln(1-y)]}{\partial u} =\frac{y}{1-y},\quad \frac{\partial y}{\partial u}=y.\] \[\boxed{\bar n_{\rm BE}(\epsilon)=\frac1{e^{\beta(\epsilon-\mu)}-1}}.\]

For a finite-temperature ideal Bose gas, the fugacity must satisfy \(\mu<\epsilon_0\), where \(\epsilon_0\) is the lowest one-particle energy, to keep the geometric sums finite. In thermodynamic-limit BEC the chemical potential approaches \(\epsilon_0\) from below.

07.3 · Fermions

Derive Fermi–Dirac occupation

SI units: Fermi energy \(\epsilon_F\) in \(\mathrm{J}\); mean occupation \(\bar n_{\rm FD}\) dimensionless and bounded by one per spin-orbital.

Grand sum over occupancy zero or one

For one fermionic orbital the allowed occupancies are 0 and 1, so

\[\Xi_\epsilon=1+y,\qquad y=e^{-\beta(\epsilon-\mu)}.\]

Differentiate the logarithm:

\[\bar n=\frac{\partial\ln(1+y)}{\partial(\beta\mu)}=\frac{y}{1+y}.\] \[\boxed{\bar n_{\rm FD}(\epsilon)=\frac1{e^{\beta(\epsilon-\mu)}+1}}.\]

The plus sign reflects Pauli exclusion: no single spin-orbital can accommodate two identical fermions.

Zero-temperature Fermi sea

At fixed chemical potential \(\mu\), take \(T\to0\) or \(\beta\to\infty\). If \(\epsilon<\mu\), \(e^{\beta(\epsilon-\mu)}\to0\) and \(\bar n\to1\). If \(\epsilon>\mu\), the exponential diverges and \(\bar n\to0\).

\[\boxed{\bar n_{\rm FD}(\epsilon)\to \begin{cases}1,&\epsilon<\epsilon_F,\\0,&\epsilon>\epsilon_F,\end{cases}}\]

At zero temperature the chemical potential approaches the Fermi energy \(\epsilon_F\) of the fixed-density gas. Therefore the many-body ground state fills multiple single-particle orbitals rather than placing all fermions in the lowest orbital.

07.4 · Classical limit

Maxwell–Boltzmann from Bose and Fermi formulas

SI units: Fugacity \(z\), occupation \(\bar n\), and \(n\lambda_T^d/g_s\) dimensionless; density \(n\) in \(\mathrm{m^{-d}}\), thermal wavelength \(\lambda_T\) in \(\mathrm{m}\).

Show why the ±1 becomes negligible

Write \(y=e^{-\beta(\epsilon-\mu)}=ze^{-\beta\epsilon}\). The bosonic and fermionic mean occupancies are \(\bar n_{\rm BE}=y/(1-y)\) and \(\bar n_{\rm FD}=y/(1+y)\).

When \(y\ll1\), expand \(1/(1\mp y)=1\pm y+O(y^2)\). To leading order,

\[\boxed{\bar n_{\rm MB}\approx y=e^{-\beta(\epsilon-\mu)}}.\]

The classical regime is controlled by low orbital occupancies and \(n\lambda_T^d/g_s\ll1\) in a dilute homogeneous gas—not by merely setting the temperature to some arbitrary large numerical value. This is a mean orbital occupation, not the normalized probability of a complete canonical microstate; see the Boltzmann-distribution derivation.

Comparison

\[\boxed{\bar n(\epsilon)= \begin{cases} e^{-\beta(\epsilon-\mu)},&\mathrm{MB\ (dilute)},\\[3pt] (e^{\beta(\epsilon-\mu)}-1)^{-1},&\mathrm{BE},\\[3pt] (e^{\beta(\epsilon-\mu)}+1)^{-1},&\mathrm{FD}. \end{cases}}\]

07.5 · Thermodynamic sums

Particle number, energy and density of states

SI units: Density of states \(g(\epsilon)\) in \(\mathrm{J^{-1}}\), differential \(d\epsilon\) in \(\mathrm{J}\); \(N\) a dimensionless count, internal energy \(U\) in \(\mathrm{J}\).

For mean occupation \(\bar n(\epsilon)\), the ideal-gas sums are \(N=\sum_j\bar n_j\), \(U=\sum_j\epsilon_j\bar n_j\). In a large system, introduce a density of states \(g(\epsilon)\) that includes spin degeneracy:

\[\boxed{N=\int_0^\infty g(\epsilon)\bar n(\epsilon)\,d\epsilon},\quad \boxed{U=\int_0^\infty \epsilon\,g(\epsilon)\bar n(\epsilon)\,d\epsilon}.\]

For bosons, treat a macroscopically occupied ground state separately when necessary: \(N=N_0+N_{\rm ex}\). For fermions in three dimensions at \(T=0\), \(N=\int_0^{\epsilon_F}g(\epsilon)\,d\epsilon\).

Worked derivation · \(U=\frac35N\epsilon_F\) in a 3D Fermi gas at \(T=0\)

For a spin-degenerate, nonrelativistic uniform 3D gas, \(g(\epsilon)=C\epsilon^{1/2}\) where \(C\) includes the volume and spin degeneracy. At zero temperature, occupations equal one for \(\epsilon<\epsilon_F\).

\[N=C\int_0^{\epsilon_F}\epsilon^{1/2}d\epsilon =\frac{2C}{3}\epsilon_F^{3/2},\] \[U=C\int_0^{\epsilon_F}\epsilon^{3/2}d\epsilon =\frac{2C}{5}\epsilon_F^{5/2}.\]

Divide the equations:

\[\boxed{U=\frac35N\epsilon_F}.\]

This is a many-particle quantum ground-state energy, not a zero-energy classical gas. The specific heat tends to zero as \(T\to0\), despite finite \(U\).

Answer unit check: \(g(\epsilon)d\epsilon\) counts states; \(U=3N\epsilon_F/5\) has units \(\mathrm{J}\).

07.6 · Extension

Bose–Einstein condensation in 3D

SI units: Critical temperature \(T_c\) in \(\mathrm{K}\), mass \(m\) in \(\mathrm{kg}\), density \(n\) in \(\mathrm{m^{-3}}\), \(\hbar\) in \(\mathrm{J\,s}\), and \(\zeta(3/2)\) dimensionless.

Sketch the ideal Bose-gas critical temperature

For a 3D uniform ideal gas of spinless bosons with ground energy set to zero, the maximum number of particles occupying excited states at temperature \(T\) occurs as \(\mu\to0^-\):

\[N_{\rm ex,max}=\frac V{\lambda_T^3}\zeta(3/2).\]

The onset of condensation occurs when \(N=N_{\rm ex,max}\). With \(n=N/V\) and \(\lambda_T=h/\sqrt{2\pi mk_BT}\), solve for \(T\):

\[\boxed{T_c=\frac{2\pi\hbar^2}{mk_B}\left(\frac n{\zeta(3/2)}\right)^{2/3}}.\]

Below \(T_c\), the excess particles occupy the lowest state macroscopically: \(N_0=N-N_{\rm ex}\) in the thermodynamic limit. This formula assumes a uniform, noninteracting, three-dimensional, spinless gas. The corresponding homogeneous ideal Bose gas does not have conventional finite-temperature BEC in 1D or 2D in the thermodynamic limit.

Answer unit check: The quantity \(\hbar^2n^{2/3}/(mk_B)\) has units \(\mathrm{K}\), as required for \(T_c\).
